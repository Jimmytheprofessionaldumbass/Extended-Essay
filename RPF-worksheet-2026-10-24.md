## What to keep, merge, or cut

### Move 1 — the narrowing decision → keep sentences 1–6 (161 w)

Sentences 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 all serve the same point and can compress to about 130 words: the first
question was rejected because it "would have resulted in a paper that is essentially a compilation
of all possible factors rather than a meaningful research" (his sentence 4 — the best sentence in
the draft, keep it nearly intact).

**Sentences 5 and 6 need merging.** "I did research regarding all possible factors…" followed by
"And wrote in the introduction about different models" is one action split across two sentences,
and sentence 6 is a fragment that starts with "And".

**Question this move must answer:** *what did rejecting the broad question teach you about what a
research question has to be?* The material is already in his sentence 4 — the paper he did not
want to write. He needs one sentence saying what he now does differently when he meets a broad
question.

### Move 2 — the failure → keep 15, 16, 17, 18 (106 w)

- 15 ("The experiment that was written had a lot of issues") — keep as the topic sentence, but
  it is 10 words saying "there were problems". It can carry the evaluative weight instead: name
  that the design was decided before the method literature was read.
- 16 (48 w) — the substance: the sensor locking, nullifying the data. Keep, trim the technical
  restatement.
- 17 (31 w) — the qualitative observation that survived. Keep.
- 18 (17 w) — the new sensor, not yet used. Keep, one clause. It must stay in the past/present —
  **do not let it imply results exist.**

**Question this move must answer:** *whose decision was the flaw?* His sentence 9 already contains
the honest answer — "I should have looked a bit more into the experiment and adapted my experiment
into it". That is the single most valuable line in the whole draft for criterion E, and it is
currently buried inside a sentence about bearing screws. Move that admission to where it is doing
the work.

### Move 3 — what survived → keep 11 and 13 (89 w)

- 11 (40 w) — the finger is not a constant material. Keep the idea, cut the hedging ("Though it
  does raise another question, being that…").
- 13 (49 w) — the ethanol result not reproducing. Keep: this is a real specific example.
- 12 (25 w) — the literature claim that alcohol removes oil. **Cut or reduce to a clause**; it is
  background, and 11 and 13 already carry the point.
- 14 ("I think I will need to mention this into my research paper", 12 w) — **cut.** It is a note
  to himself, not reflection.

**Question this move must answer:** *how did discovering that the finger is a variable change the
way you design an experiment?* Not "what did you find" — what does he now control for that he
previously assumed away.

### Move 4 — transfer → nothing exists yet, write ~100 words

Sentences 19, 20, 21 (111 w) are all forward plans — what he will do next, what he will ask his
supervisor, what further experiment he might run. **These are not transfer of learning.** They are
a to-do list, and criterion E does not ask for one.

Cut all three, and use the space for the sentence the criterion names explicitly: which capability
from this process he will carry into university work. The honest candidate is the one he already
demonstrated — designing around what is measurable, having started with a question that was not.

**Question this move must answer:** *name one thing you will do differently in the next piece of
research you are handed, and why this project is the reason.*

## The one thing that decides the mark

Right now 9 of 21 sentences are narrative-only and exactly 1 evaluates. After these cuts the ratio
improves automatically, but it only *passes* if each of the four moves ends on a judgement rather
than an event. The test for every paragraph: **does the last sentence say something about him as a
learner, or about what happened?** If it is the second, the paragraph is not finished.
