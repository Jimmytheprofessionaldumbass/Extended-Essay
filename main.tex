\section{Introduction}

    Friction is a topic that has intrigued people for millenia, and pioneered to this very day. Think honestly, what causes friction? Small microscopic asperities? Then how does perfectly smooth rubber still present friction? and why would jagged ice be so slippery? Is water the issue? Then why can you tread on water? From scientists, control engineers, and frankly, scholars that experienced its’ effects in other forms, are all trying to understand this confounding concept. 

    In particular, dry friction sometimes results in behaviours such as doors creaking, violin vibrations, etc. The oscillating change between kinetic and static friction in a motion. Take for instance a mass pulled by a spring moving at constant velocity $v_{p}$ as illustrated in figure \ref{fig:spring mass}. In the beginning, the mass is stationary with an increasing applied force from the spring towards the right with static friction of equal magnitude pulling the opposite direction, once the elastic force exceeds the static friction force, accelerating the mass forward before stopping and looping back at to the beginning with elastic force being lower than the static friction.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.7\textwidth]{spring mass system}
    \caption{Mass being pulled by a spring at constant velocity \parencite{p:rvstlg}}
    \label{fig:spring mass}
\end{figure}

    It is a topic fixated by engineers in trying to reduce, common solutions include adding grease, oil, etc. However, in the realm of competitive Minecraft, there exists many instances where the desirable effect of clicking fast can be applied by exploitation of slip stick atop the mouse. This is referred to in the community as “drag clicking”.
    Particularly, the process of drag clicking works as following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item You have your finger press down at the mouse but not clicking it because your force is not enough to overcome the static frictional force. This is will be regarded as the "stick phase"
    \item After applying enough force, your finger overcomes the static frictional force. As a result of the increase in net force and you overcome the static frictional force, your finger increasese in momentum as a result of the equation $J=F\delta t$ in the formula booklet, and as a result, this sudden increase in momentum allows for you to both click the mouse and swings in the air and moves forward by a tiny amount. This phase is seen as the "slip" phase.
    \item Your finger lands back on the mouse and reaches static friction once again, back to the beginning of the cycle

\end{enumerate}

    In this paper, we will investigate how different materials with different LuGre parameters can affect properties of slip stick friction between finger and surfaces. Such properties include cycle frequency, critical force to enter slip-stick oscillations, as well as other observations of interest.

\subsubsection{Static Friction}
    Static friction describes the maximum frictional force that is reached at rest. When applied force is beyond this threshold, the friction shifts from static friction to kinetic friction (lower than static friction). The process of this shift is often the exciter for slip stick friction.

\subsubsection{The Stribeck Effect}
The Stribeck Effect suggests that static friction does not instantaneously jump to kinetic/viscour friction but happens gradually. The rate of friction mode change depends much on material properties and in particular, we expect sharper jumps to exhibit superior drag-clicking capabilities. This means to comprehend this we cannot use coulomb friction's model as it does not consider friction as a function of velocity and is problematic because it allows the following interpretation:
\par
    Suppose the case where the applied force $F_{A} = 2N$ is less than maximum static friction $F_{S\_max} = 4N$, the opposite force currently would be 2N and the net force $F_{net} = F_{A} - F_{S} = 0$ would result in no motion.
\par
    According to the coulomb's model, the instant at which the applied force $F_{A} > F_{S}$ the system will shift into kinetic friction. For the instance that $F_{A} = 4N$ increases to $F_{A} = 4.01N$, it shifts immediately to kinetic friction, which is lower than static friction. If kinetic friction $F_{k} = 2N$ then the net force increases from $F_{net} = F_{A} - F_{S\_max} = 0$ for $F_{A} = 4N$ to $F_{net} = F_{A} - F_{k} = 4.01 - 2 = 2.01N$ for $F_{A} = 4.01N$ despite the tiny change in applied force.
    However, in real life, it is not possible because some guys did a research and tried to immediately change the force from 4.01 to 2.01N during the transition to kinetic friction and was able to achieve a net force of 0.01N which suggests the transition is not an immediate process.
\paragraph{The reason} why a steeper slope would be more desired for drag clicking is contrasted between the two cases below:
If the jump between static friction and kinetic friction were more steep (such as the optimal case of coulomb friction), then the increase in net force would be more drastic and this drastic change allows the finger to experience instant acceleration upon release, giving the finger enough momentum to click and swing forward to the next cycle.  
    However, if the transition were less instant, for example when applied force was 4.01N and after 10 microseconds the finger has accelerated to a velocity of 50cm/s and at that velocity the friction is 3N, we would have a net force of 1.01N rather than the optimal case described in the coulomb friction (of which more closely resembles the desired steep curve).
    This is supported by \parencite{woodhouse2025rosin} as violin rosin is known for its effective slip stick properties. This research discovered a large difference between kinetic coefficient of frictionand static coefficient of friction, suggesting that a large difference between kinetic and static coefficients is the cause for better slip stick properties rather than a simple increase in both coefficients.
    Conversely, \parencite{lian2021transition} states that oil reduces the difference between static and kinetic friction, and reduce or eliminates slip-stick friction.


\subsection{State Space Equations}

Consider the condition at which the same velocity and normal force results in different frictional forces \parencite{p:dm}. This is because of the dependence of friction on previous states.

While the standard textbook definition for friction stem from rigid body microscopic asperities, the Dahl Model instead visualizes two bodies making contact from elastic bristles scattered over the contacting surface\cite{p:dm}. Illustrated in figure \ref{fig:bristles}. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \center
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{bristles}
    \caption{Depiction of Dahl Model's Bristles \cite{p:dm}}
    \label{fig:bristles}
\end{figure}

For instance, if the velocity and normal force be the same, having the bristles against the direction of motion would result in larger frictional force than if the bristles were aligned with the direction of motion. 

The amount of displacement is represented by the state variable $z = \frac{F}{\sigma_{0}}$, where $\sigma_{0}$ is the stiffness of the bristles and, F is the frictional force. This can be perceived as analogous to the elastic force equation in $F_{H} = -kx$.

To coherently account for behaviour involving state changes as a result of internal states, we must shift to dynamic models that uses state space equations as it accounts for hysteresis behaviour\parencite{youtube:sseq}.

\paragraph{In state space equations,}the derivative of state variable is defined as a function of state variables, such as the LuGre model:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{z} = v - h\left(v\right)z
\end{equation}
which suggests that the rate of change of the bristle deflection is the difference between the velocity and the previous bristle deflection with regards to a function of velocity. Plugging in all known values and integrating this function over a tiny unit of time gives us the state at the next instant of the bristle deflection $z$\parencite{youtube:sseq}. Now that we have obtained both $\dot{z}$ and $z$ with a known $v$ we can plug into the second equation of the lugre model
\begin{equation}
    F = \sigma_{0}z+\sigma_{1}\dot{z} + f(v)
\end{equation}

As a result it gives us a prediction of the state successively to give a sequential account of the predicted friction. And looking at the relationship between variables in a state space equation can describe the stability of a system \parencite{youtube:sseq}, which is particularly useful in stick--slip motion.



 
\subsection{The Dahl Model} 


% Dahl Model Math -----------------------------------------------------------
Since the Dahl Model was built on the concept of elastic bristles, the foundation was based on the stress strain curve:
\begin{equation}
    F=F_{c}\left(1-e^{-\frac{ \sigma_{0}|x| }{ F_{c} }}\right)\mathrm{sgn}\left(\frac{\diff x}{\diff t}\right)
    \label{eq:dahlexp}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item $F_{c}$ is the coulomb friction, represented here as the maximum friction boundry
    \item $x$ is the displacement between surfaces similar to that in $F_{H} = -kx$, though, in this model it focuses primarily on microscopic pre-displacement. As $x$ increases, $e^{-\frac{\sigma_{0}|x|}{F_{c}}}$ decreases, which makes sense because by applying more force, predisplacement $x$ increases which results in more opposing frictional force.
    \item $\sigma_{0}$ is as aforementioned the stiffness of the bristles, analogous to the spring constant $k$ in the Physics Formula Booklet. Similar to $x$, if $\sigma_{0}$ is larger, it means that bristles are more stiff, and makes $e^{-\frac{\sigma_{0}|x|}{F_{c}}}$ easier to approach 0, making it easier to reach maximum friction.
\end{itemize}

Since state space equations are more concerned with the change in state variables, and are defined as derivatives, we must find the derivative of $F$ with respect to $x$ using chain rule in order to reveal how the force changes as the predisplacement $x$ changes:
\begin{align}
    \frac{\diff F}{\diff x} &= \cancel{-}\cancel{F_{c}}\cdot e^{-\frac{\sigma_{0}|x|}{F_{c}}}\cdot \cancel{-}\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\cancel{F_{c}}}\mathrm{sgn}\left(\frac{\diff x}{\diff t}\right)\mathrm{sgn}\left(x\right) \\
                            &= \sigma_{0}e^{-\frac{\sigma_{0}|x|}{F_{c}}}\mathrm{sgn}\left(\frac{\diff x}{\diff t}\right)\mathrm{sgn}\left(x\right)
\end{align}

\noindent
now we must put the current force state back into this derivative function, because state space equations depend on previous states as they are are history-dependent models, in this case the current friction depends on previous friction, wait, aren't we talking about presliding displacements to be the thing that is supposed to be derived, why are we particularly putting friction back, we rearrange eq (1) to isolate $e^{-\frac{\sigma_{0}|x|}{F_{c}}}$ like such:

\begin{align}
    F&=F_{c}\left(1-e^{-\frac{ \sigma_{0}|x| }{ F_{c} }}\right)\mathrm{sgn}\left(\frac{\diff x}{\diff t}\right) \\
    \frac{F}{F_{c}} \mathrm{sgn}\left(\frac{\diff x}{\diff t}\right) &= 1-e^{-\frac{\sigma_{0}|x|}{F_{c}}} \\
    e^{-\frac{\sigma_{0}|x|}{F_{c}}} &= 1 - \frac{F}{F_{c}} \mathrm{sgn}\left(\frac{\diff x}{\diff t}\right)
\end{align}
and putting it back in the differential equation $\frac{\diff F}{\diff x} = \sigma_{0}e^{-\frac{\sigma_{0}|x|}{F_{c}}}\mathrm{sgn}\left(\frac{\diff x}{\diff t}\right)\mathrm{sgn}\left(x\right)$ we get:

\begin{align}
    \frac{\diff F}{\diff x} &= \sigma_{0}\left(1-\frac{F}{F_{c}}\right)\cancel{\mathrm{sgn}\left(\frac{\diff x}{\diff t}\right)^{2}}\mathrm{sgn}\left(x\right) \\
    &=\sigma_{0}\left(1-\frac{F}{F_{c}}\right)\mathrm{sgn}\left(x\right)
\end{align}

\section{Experiment}

\subsection{Drag clicking specific methods}
There exists several known methods in the competitive minecraft community to increase the accessibility of drag clicking on a mouse. Guides on youtube on achieving drag clicking are often populated methods such as applying "tape" to your mouse \parencite{dernoze_dragclick}, some additionally suggest sharpie markers filling the surface of the tape or the mouse\parencite{deznore_dragclick}, then the mention of keeping your hands dry to ensure consistency. Several other methods were discovered with potential to achieve drag clicking. Additive methods refer to the deposition of material while subtractive likely refers to the removal of material. This is important because it helps isolate the cause of drag clicking, demonstrated below 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Additive methods
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Rubbing grinded cello rosin dust onto the surface of the mouse with your finger. Rosin is known to increase slip stick properties between a stringed instrument and a bow. From the perspective of additive, it is perceived as augumenting friction as a result of depositing a material known with a large difference between static and kinetic coefficients of friction \parencite{woodhouse2025rosin}. % The fact that it is impossible to enter kinetic friction in the experiments suggest that adding a material with stronger potentials for slip stick is more effective than removing material that hinder it, for instance in the PC plastic case where its surface oil and the finger's sebum were removed with ethanol, it was relatively easy to enter either static or kinetic friction but much more difficult in sustaining the cycle between smaller distances.
            \item Filling the surface of the mouse/tape with a sharpie. The reason this works is likely because Sharpie markers are alcolhol based \parencite{sharpie_faq}, and since alcolhol dissolves oil \parencite{saintleger1985sebum,bommannan1991ethanol} and lipids of palmer surfaces that reduces the difference between kinietic and static friction coefficient, effectively increasing the difference between kinetic and static friction.
            \item Placing electrical tape on the mouse with the non-sticky surface to be in contact with the finger. This replaces the rigid plastic surface of the mouse with a more elastically compliant surface, allowing for deformations for building up force before the slip happens, and since it is more elastically compliant, it is easier for to reset back to stick as it becomes easier for the surface to catch the finger upon landing back on the mouse surface after slip.
        \end{itemize}
    \item Subtractive methods
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Rubbing the surface of the mouse with a plant leaf. This likely works due to the oil absorbant nature of plants' leaves \parencite{gorb2017oil}\parencite{ghalia2026posidonia}, removing the reduction of the difference between kinetic and static friction, more easily allowing for drag clicking.
            \item Rubbing the surface of the mouse with the bottom of a mouse pad. This is likely due to the same phenomenon for subtractive methods as mentioned for the plant leaf method of removing excess oil to increase difference between kinetic and static friction.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Research Hypothesis and Variables}
    The difference between kinetic and static friction changes depending on material surface. This investigation aims to determine how material surface affects the difference between kinetic and static coefficient of friction.
    It is hypothesized that Cello rosin will have the largest change due to its sticky nature, and clear PC plastic is expected with the smallest change in coefficient of friction between kinetic and static due to the earlier mentioned argument contrasting the rigid plastic surface of a mouse with the elastically compliant surface of the electrical tape.
    The independent variables are clear pc, cello rosin, and sharpie while the independent variable is the difference between static and coulomb coefficient of friction.

One consideration to take in account is the finger used for the experiment. \parencite{Tomlinson2009} suggests that upon repeated testings that the middle finger of the writing hand is most reliable in applying consistent normal force for a similar setup.

The experimental procedure upon procuring this setup is:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Clean the surface with alcolhol, or reapply rosin, sharpie will not be reapplied upon initial application
    \item Clean the finger with alcolhol, or reapply rosin, again sharpie is neglected
    \item Tare the scale and while at null force, tare the force gauge
    \item Insert the string into the pulley
    \item Tape finger onto strting
    \item Place finger on the scale with no tangential force, though normal force is fine
    \item While recording slow motion, ensure visibility:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Finger state (slip/stick)
        \item Force gauge value
        \item Scale value
    \end{enumerate}
    \item Start the motor
    \item repeat steps 1-8 3 trialsa
    \item repeat for different materials
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Observations}
The electrical tape was unable to enter kinetic friction, this is likely due to the fact that that the $\alpha$ in the lugre model is too large, meaning the transition to coulomb friction was too rapid (i.e. any increase in velocity resulted in a rapid decrease of frictional force to keep from sliding). It could be possible for the finger to indeed be in motion, except that the velocity is close to zero, explaining the seeming impossibility of reaching visible coulomb friction. Since the tape is viscoelastic, it has a strong elastic spring property, that like described in figure \ref{fig:sscurve}, the resultant frictional force is directly proportional to displacement, of which is a result of applied force, which explains the difficulty in achieving kinetic friction, and once the applied force reaches the breaking point, it slips. the addition of the elasticity of the finger introduces additional difficulty in reaching kinetic coulomb friction since elastic on elastic contact gives extra opportunity for the reaction force to reflect the applied force.

For finger systems, it could be suggested that a modified lugre model should be used in which the $\sigma_{0}$ in $z=\frac{F}{\sigma_{0}}$ reflects the spring constant of a finger rather than microscopic bristles.

The trials of rosin exhibited slip stick of short slip distance before immediately sticking and slipping again. This could be a result of the material being non-viscoelastic, as such, the friction did not have the double spring effect, otherwise it would have doubled the spring of finger and material's displacement before finally being able to slip. Instead, it immediately jumped to coulomb in the case of sufficient applied force. This would be the result of a high $\alpha$ in lugre model's variation of coulomb friction as a result of velocity described in g(v). And this could be a result of particulate friction that has not been looked into in this research, but the stickiness of rosin dust on the finger could be responsible for replacing the skin sebum from contacting the surface of the rosin on the pc board, and instead change into rosin on rosin contact. The fact that it is impossible to enter kinetic friction in the experiments suggest that adding a material with stronger potentials for slip stick is more effective than removing material that hinder it, for instance in the PC plastic case where its surface oil and the finger's sebum were removed with ethanol, it was relatively easy to enter either static or kinetic friction but much more difficult in sustaining the cycle between smaller distances.

\subsection{Limitations of the data}
The force sensor used was for measuring masses and would stop updating when no change is detected. This significantly impacted the accuracy of the data. Further improvement in methodology could involve high frequency force meter and scale that directly ouput into computer for high frequency data collection, in particular a computer with high monitor refreshrate and subsequently a better slow motion camera can capture much more details entailing the phases of stick slip behavior.
Another limitation is the subject's muscle contraction when reseting, it is possible that force is applied prior to place the finger at the end for the string to not fall out of the pulley. It is also possible for the subject to unintentionally produce opposite force in the same direction of the frictional force to maximize slip stick behavior. Perhaps a future experiment with a moving surface rather than a moving finger would be used instead similar to \cite{p:ssf}

Another systemic error arises from the lack of consideration of the string sag, because the string itself has mass, and the forces measured were small, the string could impose unwanted forces. \parencite{huang2021cabledriven} suggests that this error is not a fixed additive constant, instead it is represented by the equation $F_{measured} = T \cos \theta$ where $\theta$ is the "misalignment between the load-cell axis and the cable". Due to a lack of time, this system was not implemented to fix it.
